ChatGPT is a remarkably powerful thinking partner for research and analysis — not because it has access to real-time data (it doesn't), but because it can structure problems, generate hypotheses, synthesize information you provide, and pressure-test your thinking at the speed of conversation. These prompts show you how.

> **Important caveat:** ChatGPT's knowledge has a training cutoff. For any factual claim, verify with primary sources. Use these prompts for structure, frameworks, and synthesis — not as your sole source of facts.

## Research Framework Prompts

### Prompt 1: Research Question Sharpener

```
Help me sharpen this research question:

Rough question: "[your initial question]"
Purpose: [what decision this research will inform]
Audience for the findings: [who will use this research]
Time available: [X days/weeks]
Resources: [what data or tools I have access to]

Transform my rough question into:
1. A precise, researchable question
2. 3 sub-questions that together answer the main question
3. Key variables or concepts to define
4. What a good answer to this question would look like
5. What this research will NOT attempt to answer (scope boundary)
6. Recommended research method(s) for this question type
```

---

### Prompt 2: Research Plan Builder

```
Create a research plan for:

Research question: "[your question]"
Timeline: [X days/weeks]
Resources: [tools, databases, people available]
Deliverable: [report / slide deck / memo / internal briefing]

Plan structure:
1. Research phases (with time allocation)
2. Primary vs. secondary research split
3. Sources to consult (types, not specific URLs)
4. Data collection method
5. Analysis framework
6. Potential biases to watch for
7. Risk: what if I can't find data on [specific aspect]?
8. Format for final deliverable
```

## Competitive Analysis Prompts

### Prompt 3: Competitor Analysis Framework

```
Help me analyze a competitor:

My company: [brief description]
Competitor: [name and description]
My goal: [what I want to learn — strengths / weaknesses / strategy / positioning]

Analyze this competitor across:
1. Product/service positioning (how they describe what they do)
2. Target customer (who they're going after based on observable signals)
3. Pricing strategy (what's publicly visible)
4. Marketing channels (where they appear)
5. Content strategy (what they publish and why)
6. Apparent strengths (what they do well)
7. Apparent weaknesses or gaps (where they're vulnerable)
8. Strategic implications for us (what this means for our decisions)

Label what's observable fact vs. inference vs. speculation — be explicit.
```

---

### Prompt 4: PESTLE Analysis

```
Conduct a PESTLE analysis for:

Industry: [your industry]
Geography: [country or region]
Time horizon: [next 1–3 years]

For each factor provide 3–5 specific, concrete points (not generic observations):

Political: [regulations, trade policies, political stability]
Economic: [GDP, inflation, interest rates, consumer spending]
Social: [demographic shifts, cultural changes, consumer attitudes]
Technological: [relevant tech developments, digital transformation, AI impact]
Legal: [regulations, compliance requirements, legal trends]
Environmental: [climate regulations, sustainability requirements, environmental risks]

After the analysis:
- Top 3 opportunities this environment creates
- Top 3 threats to prepare for
- One "wildcard" scenario to stress-test your strategy against
```

## Synthesis and Interpretation Prompts

### Prompt 5: Research Synthesis

```
Synthesize this research for me:

[paste your notes, summaries, or data points from multiple sources]

Research question I'm answering: "[question]"
Audience for the synthesis: [who will read this]

Produce:
1. Key findings (3–5 — only what the evidence actually supports)
2. Areas of agreement across sources
3. Areas of contradiction or uncertainty
4. What the evidence doesn't tell us (gaps)
5. Confidence level for each finding (high / medium / low — with reasoning)
6. Recommended conclusions and their basis
7. What additional research would strengthen the conclusions

Label clearly: [FINDING] / [INFERENCE] / [SPECULATION]
```

---

### Prompt 6: Devil's Advocate Analysis

```
Challenge this analysis or conclusion:

My conclusion: "[what you've concluded]"
Evidence I'm basing it on: [summarize your supporting evidence]

Act as a rigorous skeptic and:
1. Identify the 3 strongest arguments AGAINST my conclusion
2. Find assumptions I'm making that might not hold
3. Suggest alternative interpretations of my evidence
4. Identify data I might be missing that could change the conclusion
5. Rate the overall strength of my conclusion (1–10) with justification

Be constructively adversarial. The goal is a stronger analysis, not agreement.
```

## Data Interpretation Prompts

### Prompt 7: Survey Data Interpreter

```
Help me interpret this survey data:

Survey context: [who was surveyed, how many, when]
Survey question(s): [paste the questions]
Results: [paste the data — percentages, numbers, distributions]
What I expected to find: [your hypothesis going in]

Analyze:
1. Key patterns and what they suggest
2. Surprising or unexpected results
3. What the data does and doesn't tell us
4. Potential response biases to flag
5. What follow-up questions this data raises
6. How I should present these findings to [your audience]
7. The one most important takeaway

Keep interpretation grounded in the data. Flag when you're extrapolating.
```

---

### Prompt 8: Literature Review Summarizer

```
Summarize this body of literature on [topic]:

[paste abstracts, excerpts, or your notes from multiple sources]

Structure the summary as:
1. State of the field (what's established consensus)
2. Key debates or unresolved questions
3. Dominant methodologies or approaches
4. Recent developments or emerging directions
5. Gaps in the literature
6. Practical implications of the research
7. Sources cited (use the titles/authors I provided — do not invent citations)

Academic tone. Flag any areas where I've given you insufficient source material to draw strong conclusions.
```

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I trust ChatGPT for factual research?

Use it for structure, synthesis, and frameworks — not as a primary source of facts. ChatGPT can and does produce convincing but incorrect facts ("hallucinations"). Always verify specific claims, statistics, dates, and names against primary sources. The prompts in this guide are designed to help you organize and analyze research you gather yourself.

### What's the best way to use ChatGPT for competitive intelligence?

Feed it publicly available information you've gathered (company websites, press releases, job listings, product announcements) and ask it to synthesize and identify patterns. ChatGPT can't browse the web (without plugins), so you gather the data; it helps you make sense of it faster.

### How do I stop ChatGPT from making up citations?

Explicitly say: "Do not generate or invent any citations. Only reference sources I have provided. If you don't have sufficient information to support a claim, say so explicitly." This instruction dramatically reduces hallucinated citations.

### How useful is ChatGPT for academic research?

Very useful for: literature search strategy, research question refinement, methodology selection, synthesizing research you provide, and structuring arguments. Not useful as a source itself — it can't replace peer-reviewed literature, doesn't have access to academic databases, and its citations cannot be trusted without verification.

### What research tasks should I never fully delegate to ChatGPT?

Primary data collection (surveys, interviews, experiments), legal or regulatory analysis with real consequences, medical research interpretation that informs clinical decisions, and any situation where an undetected factual error would be seriously harmful. For these tasks, use AI as a thinking partner but maintain rigorous human verification of all outputs.
