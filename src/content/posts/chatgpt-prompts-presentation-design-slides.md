The best presentations tell a story. The worst are bullet point dumps that could have been an email. These prompts help you build presentations that make audiences lean forward — from investor pitches to boardroom reports to sales decks.

## Structure and Narrative Prompts

### Prompt 1: Presentation Narrative Architect

```
Design the narrative structure for a presentation:

Presentation goal: [what decision or action you want from the audience]
Audience: [who they are, what they care about, what they know]
Context: [when and where this is presented]
Key message: [the one thing they must remember]
Duration: [X minutes]
Slide count target: [approximately X slides]

Structure using the "Story Spine" framework:
1. ONCE UPON A TIME (current situation — the world as it is)
2. EVERY DAY (the problem or opportunity in that world)
3. UNTIL ONE DAY (your insight, solution, or change)
4. BECAUSE OF THAT (what becomes possible)
5. UNTIL FINALLY (the destination — what you're asking for)
6. AND EVER SINCE (the future state after they take action)

Map each story beat to slides:
- Slide numbers for each section
- One-line content description per slide
- The emotional moment you want to create in each section
```

### Prompt 2: Slide-by-Slide Content Generator

```
Write content for each slide of this presentation:

Presentation title: "[title]"
Audience: [description]
Outline:
[paste your slide structure or section plan]

For each slide provide:
- Slide title (under 7 words — active verb preferred)
- Main content (3 bullet points MAX — or 1 headline stat — or 1 key visual description)
- Speaker note (what to SAY that isn't on the slide — 2–4 sentences)
- Visual recommendation (what image, chart, or icon would work here)

Slide rules I'm following:
- One idea per slide
- No complete sentences on slides
- Speaker notes contain the full story
- Slides are a visual aid, not a script
```

### Prompt 3: Data Visualization Brief

```
Create a data visualization brief for this data:

Data: [paste your numbers or describe the dataset]
Point I want to make: [the insight this data should communicate]
Audience sophistication: [no data background / business / technical]
Presentation context: [boardroom slide / infographic / dashboard / report]

For each data point, recommend:
1. Chart type and why (bar / line / pie / scatter / table / single big number)
2. What to highlight or annotate
3. What to remove (data that muddies the message)
4. The headline for this chart (what to title it so the insight is obvious)
5. Color coding recommendation (what should be red / green / highlighted)

Rule: every chart should make one point clearly. If it shows two things, make two charts.
```

## Slide Copy Prompts

### Prompt 4: Slide Headline Optimizer

```
Rewrite these slide headlines to be stronger:

Current headlines:
1. [current slide title]
2. [current slide title]
3. [current slide title]
4. [current slide title]
5. [current slide title]

For each, write:
- A stronger version using an active verb
- A "so what?" test (does the headline communicate the takeaway, not just the topic?)

Example of weak vs strong:
Weak: "Market Analysis" → Strong: "Our market is growing 40% annually"
Weak: "Team" → Strong: "This team has built and sold two companies before"

Rewrite all my headlines using this principle.
```

### Prompt 5: Presentation Speaker Notes

```
Write speaker notes for these slides:

Presentation context: [what this is for, who the audience is]
My expertise on this topic: [what you know well]
Presentation style: [conversational / formal / storytelling / data-driven]
Time per slide: [approximately X seconds / minutes]

Slides:
Slide [X]: [paste slide content]
Slide [X]: [paste slide content]
Slide [X]: [paste slide content]

For each slide, write speaker notes that:
- Expand the slide content naturally (don't read the bullets aloud)
- Include 1 transition phrase from the previous slide
- Include 1 audience engagement moment (rhetorical question / pause / stat to let land)
- Stay within the time target when read at natural speaking pace

Format: ready to paste into PowerPoint/Keynote notes view.
```

## Delivery Prompts

### Prompt 6: Q&A Preparation

```
Prepare me for Q&A after presenting: [presentation topic]

Audience: [who attended]
My position: [what I presented and what I want them to believe/do]
Sensitive topics in my presentation: [anything that might draw criticism]

Generate:
1. The 10 hardest questions this audience could ask
2. Strong answers to each (concise, honest, confident)
3. "Bridge" phrases to redirect hostile questions back to my key message
4. How to handle: "I don't know the answer to that"
5. How to handle: a question that challenges my core premise
6. The question I'm most afraid of — and the best answer to it

Also: what to do when someone asks a very long multi-part question.
```

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many slides should a presentation have?

A common rule: one slide per minute of speaking time. A 20-minute presentation = roughly 20 slides. But more important than count is density: one idea per slide, not five. A 10-slide presentation with one powerful idea per slide beats a 30-slide deck where each slide has 6 bullets and a clip-art image.

### Should I put full sentences or bullet points on slides?

Neither, ideally. Slides are a visual aid for your spoken words — not a script or a document. Use: headline stats ("$47B market by 2028"), short fragments ("Revenue up 3x"), single powerful images, or one large number. Your speaker notes (Prompt 5) contain the full narrative. When audiences read your slides, they stop listening to you.

### How do I make data slides interesting?

Make one point per chart and put the conclusion in the headline (Prompt 3). "Revenue grew 47%" is a stronger slide title than "Revenue by Quarter." Highlight the one number that matters. Remove gridlines and chart junk. Use color to guide the eye to the insight. The goal is instant comprehension, not comprehensive data display.

### How do I overcome presentation nerves?

Prepare relentlessly — nervous presenters are usually undertrained presenters. Know your opening two minutes cold (practice it 20 times until it's automatic). The anxiety peaks at the start and drops once you're in flow. Also: reframe the nervousness. The physical feeling of anxiety is nearly identical to excitement — tell yourself "I'm excited" instead of "I'm nervous." Research shows this actually works.

### What's the biggest presentation mistake executives make?

Too much information trying to prove they know everything. Executives are most effective when they're the least content-dense and most conviction-clear. One key message, delivered with evidence and confidence, is more persuasive than comprehensive data coverage. Trust your audience to ask for more detail if they need it.
