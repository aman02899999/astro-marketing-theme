Negotiation outcomes are decided before you walk into the room. The prep work — knowing your position, anticipating the other side, and planning your responses — determines 80% of the result. These prompts do the prep work in minutes.

## Salary Negotiation Prompts

### Prompt 1: Salary Negotiation Script

```
Prepare a salary negotiation script for:

Job offer received: [$X base + benefits]
My target: [$Y base — or total comp target]
My current/previous salary: [$Z — or "first job"]
Competing offer: [$X from Company B — or "no competing offer"]
My leverage: [strong performance data / competing offer / specialized skills / market data]
Their likely constraints: [startup budget / corporate band / etc.]

Produce:
1. Opening statement (how to bring up the negotiation naturally)
2. My anchor (first number I say and how to frame it)
3. Justification talking points (3 evidence-based reasons for my ask)
4. Response to "That's above our budget": (2 versions — with and without competing offer)
5. Response to "This is our best offer": (how to test if it's actually final)
6. What to say if they say yes immediately (recalibrate upward)
7. Non-salary items to negotiate if salary is fixed (signing bonus / vacation / start date / title / remote work)
8. Walk-away line (professional, relationship-preserving)
```

### Prompt 2: Vendor/Supplier Negotiation

```
Prepare me for a vendor price negotiation:

Vendor: [what they supply]
Current price: [$X per unit/month]
My target: [$Y — or X% reduction]
Contract volume: [how much you buy]
Alternatives: [competing vendors or in-house option]
Relationship length: [new / existing for X years]
Their likely concerns: [losing the account / margin / precedent]

Prepare:
1. My BATNA (Best Alternative to Negotiated Agreement)
2. Their likely BATNA
3. Zone of Possible Agreement (ZOPA) — what range could both sides accept?
4. Opening position and rationale
5. Concession strategy (what to give up and in what order)
6. Pressure points I can legitimately use
7. Script for the opening conversation
8. Counters to their likely pushbacks
```

### Prompt 3: Difficult Conversation Prep

```
Prepare me for this difficult conversation:

Situation: [describe what happened and what needs to be addressed]
Who I'm talking to: [their role and my relationship to them]
What I want to achieve: [ideal outcome]
What I'm willing to accept: [minimum acceptable outcome]
What I'm NOT willing to accept: [hard limits]
Their likely reaction: [how do you expect them to respond?]
What I'm afraid might happen: [your concern]

Prepare:
1. Opening statement (clear, non-aggressive, states purpose)
2. How to present my position using facts, not feelings
3. Active listening cues (phrases to use when they push back)
4. How to handle raised voices or defensiveness
5. The sentence I should NOT say (and why)
6. Three possible paths to resolution and how to suggest each
7. How to close the conversation — regardless of outcome
```

## Partnership Negotiation Prompts

### Prompt 4: Partnership Term Sheet Prep

```
Prepare for a business partnership negotiation:

My business: [description]
Their business: [description]
Partnership type: [revenue share / equity / co-marketing / licensing / distribution]
What I bring: [your assets, audience, IP, distribution]
What they bring: [their assets]
My ideal terms: [describe what you want]
My bottom line: [minimum acceptable terms]

Produce:
1. The core value exchange (what both sides actually get)
2. Key terms to negotiate (prioritized list)
3. Standard vs. deal-specific terms (what to accept as boilerplate vs. what to fight for)
4. Common partnership pitfalls to address upfront
5. Proposed term sheet outline
6. Red flags to watch for in their counter-proposals
```

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When should I bring up salary negotiation?

Wait until you have a written offer. Never negotiate in the first interview — it signals you care more about money than the role. When asked about expectations before an offer: "I'd prefer to understand the full role and compensation package before discussing specific numbers." Once you have the offer in writing, you have maximum leverage.

### Is it always worth negotiating?

Yes, almost always. The worst outcome of a professional negotiation is hearing "no" — and you're no worse off than before you asked. Research shows that 80%+ of employers expect salary negotiation and build room into their initial offers. Not negotiating leaves money on the table in the vast majority of cases.

### How do I negotiate without a competing offer?

Use market data instead. Salary surveys (Glassdoor, Levels.fyi, Payscale, LinkedIn Salary) show market ranges for your role, level, and location. Lead with: "Based on my research of market rates for this role in [location], and given my [specific experience/skills], I believe [target] is the right number." Market data is a legitimate anchor.

### What's the most common negotiation mistake?

Talking too much after making your ask. State your number, explain why briefly, then stop talking. The uncomfortable silence that follows is the other side thinking — let them. Many negotiators undermine their own position by filling silence with concessions they didn't need to make.

### Can I negotiate after accepting an offer?

Technically yes, but it damages trust and your relationship before you start. Negotiate before accepting. If you accepted verbally, you can still negotiate before signing, but approach carefully: "I've been reflecting on the offer and I wanted to have one more conversation before I sign." Use this sparingly — it works once.
