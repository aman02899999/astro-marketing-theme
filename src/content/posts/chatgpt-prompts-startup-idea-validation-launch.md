Building a startup means making high-stakes decisions with incomplete information at high speed. These prompts help founders think faster, validate smarter, and communicate more clearly at every stage from napkin sketch to launch.

## Idea Validation Prompts

### Prompt 1: Startup Idea Stress Test

```
Stress test this startup idea:

Idea: [describe your idea in 2–3 sentences]
Target customer: [who would use/pay for this]
Problem being solved: [the specific pain]
My background: [why you're qualified to build this]
Stage: [just an idea / built prototype / have early users]

Challenge it across every dimension:

MARKET
- How large is this market realistically?
- Is this a vitamin (nice to have) or a painkiller (must have)?
- Are there enough people who have this problem AND will pay to solve it?

COMPETITION
- Why don't existing solutions solve this already?
- What happens if a well-funded competitor copies this in 6 months?

MOAT
- What makes this defensible over time?
- What's the unfair advantage I have that others don't?

BUSINESS MODEL
- How do you make money?
- What's the unit economics?
- What's the CAC/LTV story?

FOUNDER FIT
- Why are you specifically the right person to build this?

Be brutally honest. I want to find the fatal flaw before I spend a year on this.
```

### Prompt 2: Customer Discovery Interview Guide

```
Create a customer discovery interview guide for:

My hypothesis: [the assumption I'm testing — e.g. "busy parents struggle to plan nutritious meals and would pay for help"]
Target interviewee: [who to interview]
Interview goal: [validate the problem / understand current solutions / test willingness to pay]
Duration: [30–45 minutes]

Interview structure:
1. Warm-up (5 min): build rapport, understand their context
2. Problem exploration (15 min): understand the problem from their perspective
3. Current behavior (10 min): how do they solve this today?
4. Solution reaction (5 min): show concept — gauge interest without pitching
5. Closing (5 min): referrals and final questions

For each section: exact questions to ask + what a "validated" vs "invalidated" signal sounds like.
Also: 5 questions to NEVER ask in a customer discovery interview (leading questions that bias results).
```

## Product Development Prompts

### Prompt 3: MVP Feature Specification

```
Help me define the MVP for:

Product: [description]
Core problem: [what it solves]
Target user: [who uses it]
My hypothesis to test: [what you're trying to validate with the MVP]

Produce:
1. The smallest possible feature set that tests the core hypothesis
2. For each feature: user story format ("As a [user], I want to [action] so that [benefit]")
3. What to explicitly leave OUT of MVP and why
4. Success metrics (how will I know the MVP validated or invalidated the hypothesis?)
5. Timeline estimate for a 2-person team
6. Suggested tech stack (based on need for speed, not scalability)

Principle: the MVP should be embarrassingly small. If you're not embarrassed, it's too big.
```

### Prompt 4: Product Roadmap Builder

```
Build a 12-month product roadmap for:

Product: [description]
Current stage: [pre-launch / launched / post-PMF]
North star metric: [the one metric that defines success]
Team size: [X developers + roles]
Validated learnings so far: [what you know works]

Roadmap structure:
Q1: Foundation (stability, core value delivery)
Q2: Growth features (what makes users invite others)
Q3: Monetization (if not already)
Q4: Scale (handling 10x users)

For each quarter:
- 2–3 themes (not feature lists)
- Hypothesis for each theme
- Success metric
- Kill criteria (if this doesn't work, we pivot to...)

Also: the 3 features most founders add too early (avoid these in Q1).
```

## Pitch and Fundraising Prompts

### Prompt 5: Pitch Deck Outline

```
Create a pitch deck outline for:

Company: [name]
Stage: [pre-seed / seed / Series A]
Ask: [$X for Y months of runway to achieve Z milestone]
Traction: [revenue / users / pilots / LOIs — or pre-revenue]

Slide-by-slide outline (12–14 slides):
1. Title slide
2. Problem (the pain, quantified)
3. Solution (your approach)
4. Why now (tailwind, timing)
5. Product (demo or screenshots)
6. Market (TAM/SAM/SOM)
7. Business model
8. Traction (graphs > words)
9. Go-to-market
10. Competition (honest)
11. Team (why you)
12. Financials (3-year projection, key assumptions)
13. The ask (amount, use of funds, milestones)
14. Appendix (extras)

For each slide: one sentence of what to communicate + what to avoid.
```

### Prompt 6: Investor Q&A Preparation

```
Prepare me for investor Q&A:

My startup: [description]
Stage: [seed / A]
Traction: [current metrics]
The investment thesis I'm pitching: [your core story]

Generate the 20 hardest questions an experienced investor would ask me, covering:
- Why you (team)
- Why now (timing)
- Why this (market and solution)
- Unit economics and path to profitability
- Defensibility and moat
- What could kill this company
- How you'll spend the money
- Exit scenarios

For each question: the question + what a strong answer sounds like + what a weak answer looks like.
```

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I use ChatGPT without building a product nobody wants?

Use it for preparation and frameworks (Prompt 1, Prompt 2), not for replacing customer conversations. ChatGPT can help you identify what to test and how to interview — it can't replace the 20 customer discovery conversations you need to have. The founders who use AI most effectively are those who use it to prepare for real-world validation, not instead of it.

### What should an MVP actually include?

The single feature that delivers the core value proposition and lets you test the primary hypothesis. Nothing else. ChatGPT's tendency (and founders' tendency) is to add "just one more feature" to the MVP. Prompt 3's principle is right: if you're not embarrassed by how simple it is, it's probably too complex.

### When should a startup raise funding?

When you have a clear hypothesis that requires capital to test, and you've validated the problem with real users. Raising money before validation buries the learning signal under pressure to hit investor expectations. Raise after you have evidence the problem is real and people want your solution, not before.

### How do I make a pitch deck stand out?

Lead with traction, not narrative. Investors see hundreds of decks — ones that open with real revenue or real users immediately stand out from theoretical decks. If you're pre-revenue, open with the most compelling evidence that the problem is real and your team is uniquely positioned. Clarity beats design; specificity beats vision.

### What's the most common startup mistake ChatGPT can help you avoid?

Building in isolation. Use Prompt 1 (Stress Test) before writing a single line of code. Use Prompt 2 (Customer Discovery) to design your first 20 conversations. Most startups fail because they built something people didn't actually want — not because the technology was wrong. ChatGPT can stress-test your assumptions before you invest months.
