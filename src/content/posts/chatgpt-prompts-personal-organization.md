Most productivity systems fail not because the method is wrong but because it's too complex to maintain. These prompts help you build personal organization systems that fit your actual life — not an idealized version of it.

## Goal Setting Prompts

### Prompt 1: Annual Goals Design

```
Help me design my annual goals:

Current situation: [brief description of where you are in life/career]
Areas I want to improve: [list 3–5 life areas: career / health / relationships / finances / learning / etc.]
My biggest frustration right now: [honest answer]
What success looks like in 12 months: [describe your ideal situation]
Time available for focused work: [X hours/week beyond day job]

Help me:
1. Identify 3 "keystone goals" (that unlock progress in multiple areas)
2. Translate each into a SMART goal with a specific metric
3. Identify the one habit or behavior change that most enables each goal
4. Design a weekly review ritual to track progress
5. Flag any goals that conflict with each other
6. Predict: which goal is most likely to stall and why?
```

### Prompt 2: Goal Breakdown to Weekly Actions

```
Break this goal into actionable weekly steps:

Goal: [your goal]
Deadline: [date]
Current progress: [where you are now]
Resources available: [time per week, tools, support]
Known obstacles: [what might get in the way]

Output:
1. Goal broken into 4–6 milestones with dates
2. Weekly actions for the next 4 weeks (specific, not vague)
3. Leading indicators to track weekly (not just outcomes)
4. Minimum viable progress (what counts as "not falling behind")
5. Recovery plan (what to do if you miss a week)
```

## Note-Taking System Prompts

### Prompt 3: Second Brain Setup

```
Help me design a personal knowledge management system (Second Brain):

Tools I use or am considering: [Notion / Obsidian / Roam / Apple Notes / Logseq — pick yours]
Types of information I deal with: [work notes / research / book highlights / project docs / personal logs]
My biggest note-taking problem: [can't find things / never review notes / too complex to maintain / etc.]
Time I can spend on PKM per week: [X minutes]

Design a system with:
1. Capture method (how to get things in quickly)
2. Organization structure (folders, tags, or links — appropriate for my tool)
3. Processing routine (when and how to organize what you capture)
4. Review cadence (daily, weekly, monthly review habits)
5. Output method (how notes become projects, writing, or decisions)

Make it as simple as possible. I don't want to maintain a second job.
```

### Prompt 4: Meeting Notes Template

```
Design a reusable meeting notes template for:

My role: [your job]
Meeting types I attend most: [list 2–3 types]
What I need from notes: [action items / decisions / context for future reference]
Tool I take notes in: [Notion / Obsidian / Google Docs / paper]

Create a template with:
1. Header (meeting name, date, attendees — 30 seconds to fill)
2. Context (1-line purpose — fills itself once you write it once)
3. Key discussion points (bullet capture during meeting)
4. Decisions made (explicit section — prevents "I thought we decided..." later)
5. Action items (owner + task + deadline format)
6. Open questions (unresolved items)
7. Next meeting date and agenda seed

Should take under 5 minutes to fill during any meeting.
```

## Habit Design Prompts

### Prompt 5: Habit Stacking Planner

```
Design a habit stack for:

New habits I want to build: [list 3–5 habits]
Existing anchor habits (things I already do reliably): [list 3–5 daily anchors — morning coffee, brushing teeth, etc.]
My schedule: [morning person / night owl / variable]
Past habit failures: [what you've tried that didn't stick]
Time available: [X minutes per day for new habits]

Design a habit stack that:
1. Attaches each new habit to an existing anchor
2. Keeps the initial version tiny (2 minutes maximum)
3. Sequences habits logically through the day
4. Builds in a tracking method (simple, not complex)
5. Has a reset protocol for when you break the chain

Use implementation intentions: "After I [anchor habit], I will [new habit] for [X minutes]."
```

### Prompt 6: Morning Routine Designer

```
Design an optimal morning routine for:

Wake time: [what time you wake up]
Available morning time: [X minutes before commitments start]
Goals the morning should serve: [energy / focus / health / learning / creative work]
Current morning: [describe what you actually do now]
Biggest morning challenge: [hitting snooze / phone rabbit hole / rushing / etc.]

Design:
1. The ideal 30/45/60-minute morning sequence (pick one based on available time)
2. Each activity with exact duration
3. Why each activity is placed where it is
4. Phone/screen protocol for the first hour
5. A "minimum viable morning" for bad days (10 minutes only)
6. First week implementation plan (don't change everything at once)
```

## Weekly Review Prompts

### Prompt 7: Weekly Review Template

```
Create a weekly review template I'll actually use:

My role: [job title or lifestyle context]
Review goal: [stay on track / reduce anxiety / improve next week / all three]
Time available for weekly review: [X minutes]
Tool: [Notion / paper / Google Doc]

Template sections:
1. Brain dump (clear everything out — 5 minutes)
2. Last week: what went well (2–3 things)
3. Last week: what didn't go well (honest, not self-critical)
4. Completion check: did I hit my 3 priorities?
5. This week: 3 priorities (just 3 — not 10)
6. This week: any appointments or deadlines to note
7. One thing to let go of from last week

Keep under 20 minutes total. Make the questions specific enough that I can't write vague answers.
```

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do most productivity systems fail?

Complexity. People build systems more elaborate than they can maintain under stress, and the first difficult week causes a complete abandon. The most durable systems are boring: a simple capture method, a weekly review, and a short daily priority list. Everything else is optimization for later. Prompt 3 and Prompt 7 are designed around this principle.

### What's the best note-taking app for building a second brain?

The best app is the one you'll actually use. Obsidian is the most powerful for linked thinking and long-term knowledge building. Notion is most flexible for mixed content. Apple Notes is most frictionless for quick capture. Pick one and commit for 90 days before evaluating — app-switching is often procrastination in disguise.

### How do I stop setting goals and never achieving them?

Two issues typically: goals are too outcome-focused (you can't control outcomes, only actions), and there's no system connecting goals to weekly behavior. Prompt 1 and Prompt 2 together fix both — designing goals tied to weekly leading indicators you can actually control.

### How many habits should I try to build at once?

One. Seriously — one at a time, kept tiny, attached to an existing anchor, for 60 days before adding the next. This sounds slower but produces far better results than habit stacking 5 new behaviors simultaneously, which causes all 5 to fail within 3 weeks.

### What's the single most high-leverage productivity habit?

The weekly review. Most people live reactively — responding to whatever arrives. A 20-minute weekly review (Prompt 7) creates a 10-second-per-day clarity benefit by keeping your priorities visible and your commitments realistic. It's the meta-habit that makes all other habits stick.
